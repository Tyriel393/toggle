# "Make room" — concept validation

**For:** Josip, to test with freelancer/contractor friends and his wife.
**Rule:** Part 1 goes out **first, on its own.** Do not show Part 2 until they've answered. If they see the concept first they'll be polite about it and you'll learn nothing.

---

## PART 1 — send this (no concept, no context)

> Quick one — I'm looking at how freelancers handle their week. Can you think back to the **last time a client asked you for something urgent**, mid-week?
>
> 1. Do you plan your week anywhere? (Calendar, notebook, tool, head?)
> 2. What was the request, and roughly how long did it take?
> 3. How did you decide whether to say yes?
> 4. **At the moment you replied to the client — what did you actually check?** (Calendar? A tool? Nothing?)
> 5. Did anything else you had planned slip because of it?
> 6. If it did — did you realise **at the time**, or only later?
> 7. Have you ever agreed to something and then found you could not fit it?
> 8. How often does the plan in your head turn out to be wrong?
>
> No right answers, just what actually happened.

**Ask six people if you can. Three is enough to see a pattern.**

---

## PART 2 — only after they've answered

> Here's what I'm thinking about. Imagine your week is roughly laid out in a tool — a few client jobs, rough hours on each.
>
> Wednesday, a client asks for urgent homepage revisions. About 4 hours.
>
> You drop it into Wednesday. Before it's added, the tool says:
>
> **"Wednesday is 2h over. Here's what's already on it:"**
> - Northstar — Website QA · 3h · *due Thursday*
> - Portfolio update · 2h
>
> You drag the portfolio work to Friday. Wednesday fits again. The Thursday deadline is safe.
>
> That's it. It doesn't decide for you — it just shows you're over before you commit, and what's in the way.
>
> **Questions:**
> - Would that have helped in the situation you described?
> - Or would you have ignored it and worked late anyway?
> - What would make it annoying?
> - **What would you need to trust that warning?** (Deadlines? Priorities? Confidence in your own estimates?)
> - Would knowing *which* client commitment was being displaced change what you told the client asking?

## What Josip should listen for

Do not count "yeah that's useful." Count these.

### Signals the idea is real

- They describe **finding out afterwards** that something slipped
- They mention **working late / weekends** to absorb a request
- They keep **some kind of plan** — even a rough one, even on paper
- They've **agreed to something and regretted it**
- They say something like *"I never know what it costs me until it's too late"*

### Signals the idea is wrong

- **"I don't plan, I just work off a list"** ← the most likely killer. The whole concept needs a plan to exist.
- **"I just say yes and figure it out"** — no decision moment to support
- **"I keep it in my head"** — softer than it looks. Follow up: how often is the plan in your head wrong?
- **"I would ignore it and work late anyway"** — note: this proves the *warning* is weak, not that the problem is absent. Follow up with: would knowing which commitment was displaced change what you told the client?
- **"I don't estimate my work and wouldn't trust an hours-based warning"** ← the real killer. (Fixed-price work alone does NOT invalidate the idea — 4h of urgent work still displaces 4h of something, and unpaid overrun makes the cost worse.)

### The question that matters most

> **Did anything slip, and did you know at the time or only later?**

If most say **"only later"** — the idea has a real job.
If most say **"I knew, I just chose to work late"** — the warning is noise, and this direction is weaker than it looks.

---

## Honest limits

Six friends is **not** market research. It's a grounded hypothesis. If it comes up in the assignment, say exactly that:

> This came from watching one designer and a few developers work. It gave me the hypothesis, not the prevalence — I'd validate that properly before building.

That's more credible than implying it's proven, and it's the kind of distinction the evaluators are looking for.

---

## Optional: the wife version

Same questions, but ground it in something concrete rather than hypothetical:

> Show me last week. Which clients did you work for, roughly how many hours each? Was there anything you'd meant to do that didn't happen? When did you notice?

Watching someone reconstruct a real week is worth more than any hypothetical answer — and the reconstruction itself is data.
